Skill at 99¢

    Polymarket · Nov–Dec 2025 · B = 1,000 · seed 20261031

    Risk 99¢ to make 1¢. The test calls it skill.

    In short

    A trader can risk 99¢ to make 1¢ and win almost every time. A skill test from a 2026 paper calls that trader skilled. This page explains how the test works, from zero, why the price fools it, and what I found on 1,848,270 real Polymarket trades.

    Start here: the basics, one card at a time.

    What Polymarket is, what a 99¢ bet is, and how a test tries to tell skill from luck. No maths needed. Scroll to turn the cards.

    1. 1 / 9

      Polymarket is a market for yes-or-no questions.

      People trade on questions about the future, like “Will it rain in London tomorrow?”. A YES share pays $1 if the answer is yes and nothing if it is no. A NO share is the opposite.

      Each person trading is an account. Each question is an event.

    2. 2 / 9

      The price is the crowd's guess.

      The price moves until buyers and sellers agree, so it works as the crowd's estimate. YES at 70¢ means about a 70% chance of yes. At 99¢ the crowd is almost sure.

      The side with the high price is called the favourite.

    3. 3 / 9

      Buying at 99¢: many tiny wins, one big loss.

      Pay 99¢ for a share that pays $1. If the favourite wins, you make 1¢. If it loses, you lose all 99¢.

      At the right price the loss comes about once in 100 bets, and the 99 small wins pay for it exactly: the total is zero. Until that loss arrives, the trader looks like a steady winner.

    4. 4 / 9

      Skilled, or just lucky?

      Among thousands of traders, some make money by luck alone, the way one person in 1,024 flips ten heads in a row.

      In 2026 four researchers, Gómez-Cram, Guo, Jensen and Kung, asked which Polymarket traders really know more than the crowd. They needed a test that says, account by account, whether a profit is more than luck.

    5. 5 / 9

      The test: flip a coin on every result.

      Write down what one account made on each event. With no skill, each result could just as well have gone the other way. So the test flips a coin per result: heads keeps it, tails reverses it, and +3¢ becomes −3¢. The new sum is one luck-only history.

    6. 6 / 9

      The verdict is the p-value.

      The p-value is the share of luck-only histories that do as well as the real total. Under 5%, luck rarely matches it: the account is called skilled. Over 95%, it loses more than luck explains: anti-skilled. In between, no label.

      With five results the coins can land only 32 ways, and 5 of them reach +7¢: about 16%, so no label.

    7. 7 / 9

      What the paper found.

      On Polymarket trades from 2023 to 2025, the test called 8.63% of accounts skilled and 16.84% anti-skilled (Table 1 of the paper).

      So a small group wins more than luck allows, and a bigger group loses more than luck explains.

    8. 8 / 9

      What I found: the other way round.

      For my Build at TKS (The Knowledge Society), where I study quantitative finance on the blockchain, I rebuilt the test and ran it on 1,848,270 trades by 5,000 accounts, from November and December 2025.

      My result: 26.89% skilled and 11.05% anti-skilled. More skilled than anti-skilled, the reverse of the paper.

    9. 9 / 9

      Why: the coin never makes the big loss.

      Most of the gap comes from accounts that buy at 99¢. Take one with no skill over 20 events. 81.8% of the time it never loses: twenty wins of +1¢.

      The coin turns +1¢ into −1¢, never into the −99¢ of a real loss. So no luck-only history beats it, and the test calls it skilled. The next part shows it live.

    How I built the test and checked it before trusting it.

    Four steps: copy the test exactly, try it on fake traders whose right answer I knew, prepare the real data, then run it on real accounts.

    1. 01 · Read

      I wrote the test from the paper (Gómez-Cram, Guo, Jensen and Kung, 25 June 2026) as a Python program. For each account: add up the profit of every event, make 1,000 luck-only versions with a coin for each sign, and count how many do as well as the real total. The box below is the exact recipe, with the paper's own rules, for readers who want the formulas.

      +1heads keeps the profit, tails flips it: the sign each event gets, at random

    2. 02 · Check

      Before touching real data, I ran the test on fake traders I programmed myself, so I knew the right answer. Traders betting at random were called skilled 5.95% of the time, close to the 5% a fair test should give. Traders with a real edge (they win 10 points more often than the price says) needed about 160 trades before the test found them. But traders who only bought favourites at 90–99¢, with no skill at all, were called skilled 59.25% of the time. That was the first warning.

    3. 03 · Data

      The data is a public copy of every Polymarket trade: 1,266,885,846 rows, each one a match between a buyer and a seller. I kept November and December 2025, removed market makers (accounts that post offers on both sides all day to earn the gap between prices, not to bet), and drew 5,000 accounts at random. They made 1,848,270 trades.

    4. 04 · Test

      Then I ran the test on the 5,000 real accounts. If nobody had any skill, about 5% of them, around 250, would be called skilled by luck. 1,430 were. And 737 of those 1,430 belong to the 879 accounts that pay 95¢ or more per share on average. In the chart, each dot is one account: the price it pays, left to right, and its p-value, bottom to top.

    The more an account pays per share, the more often the test calls it skilled.

    I sorted the accounts into six groups by the average price they pay for a share. Under 80¢, 11.4% are called skilled. At 99¢ or more, 613 of 702 accounts are, and none of the 702 is called anti-skilled.

    Why a trader with no skill passes: the coin never produces the big loss.

    A 99¢ share wins +1¢ almost every time and loses −99¢ about once in a hundred. The coin turns a +1¢ win into a −1¢, never into −99¢. So over 20 events the luck-only histories run from −20¢ to +20¢, and a trader who never lost sits at the very top. At 99¢ that happens 81.8% of the time, with no skill.

    How to read it: I simulated 4,000 traders with no skill for each price and number of events. Each pile is one price (left to right) and one number of events (front to back), and its height is the share the test calls skilled. The green plane at 5% is what a fair test would give. The higher the price, the further the piles rise above it.

    Leave out the expensive shares and my result moves back toward the paper.

    A price cap keeps only the accounts whose average price is under a limit. With no cap, 26.9% of accounts are skilled and 11.1% anti-skilled, the reverse of the paper. With the cap at 80¢ the two come close: 11.4% and 14.1%.

"Raw p" is the test as it is.     Four checks on where the "skill" comes from.

    Each check asks the same question in a different way: does the label follow the trader, or the price the trader pays?

    1. A · Against the paper

      With every account in, I find about three times the paper's share of skilled accounts (26.89% against 8.63%) and two-thirds of its share of anti-skilled ones (11.05% against 16.84%). Leave out the accounts that pay 80¢ or more on average and the order is the paper's again: fewer skilled than anti-skilled.

    2. B · Against a trader with no skill

      For each price group I simulated traders with no skill at the same prices. In five of the six groups, in both periods, real accounts are called skilled about as often as the simulated ones (inside the grey range). Only 80–90¢ is above it: 42.2% against at most 35.2%. That is the one group where real accounts beat what luck gives.

    3. C · Against the correction

      Benjamini–Hochberg is the standard correction when thousands of accounts are tested at once: it removes the labels luck alone would hand out. It removes 53.1% of the anti-skilled labels but only 10.3% of the skilled ones (37.8% and 52.3% under an 80¢ cap). It can remove lucky passes; it cannot fix luck-only histories that leave out the big loss.

    4. D · Where the labels come from

      Accounts that pay 99¢ or more on average are 14.5% of the buyers but produce 47% of all skilled labels in November and December. In December alone they are 19.7% of the buyers and produce 53.4%.

    Verdict

    On these trades, the test mostly measures the price a trader pays, not how good the trader is.

    1. 87.3%

      of accounts that pay 99¢ or more are flagged skilled, close to the 82.7% a trader with no skill gets in simulation at 99¢ and 20 events.

    2. 5 / 6

      price groups, in both periods, sit inside the range of traders with no skill: the labels are what luck-only histories give when the loss is this rare and this large.

    3. 47%

      of all skilled labels come from the 14.5% of buyers who pay 99¢ or more; the standard correction barely touches them.

    4. 0.81

      skilled accounts for every anti-skilled one when the shares over 80¢ are left out. With every account in it is 2.43; in the paper, 0.51. Without the expensive shares, my result moves toward the paper.

    So counting "skilled" accounts on Polymarket mostly counts how many favourites they buy. This does not prove the paper wrong: I used two months instead of three years, and 5,000 accounts. It shows that the result needs the prices next to it, and that the test needs a version that knows a 99¢ share can lose 99¢.
