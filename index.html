Skill at 99¢

    Polymarket · Nov–Dec 2025 · B = 1,000 · seed 20261031

    Risk 99¢ to make 1¢. The test calls it skill.

    Twenty bets, replayed 1,000 times with their signs flipped.

    Each bill is one history: the same 20 profits, every sign flipped at random. Piles count histories by total profit. The test calls a trader skilled when fewer than 5% of histories beat the real record. Hover a pile to read it.

    The route: read the paper, prove the test on traders whose answer is known, then cut 1.27 billion fills down to 5,000 accounts.

    1. 01 · Read

      The test comes from Gómez-Cram, Guo, Jensen and Kung (25 June 2026): give each event's profit a random sign, 1,000 times, and count how often chance beats the real record.

      +1the sign this event's profit gets, flipped at random

    2. 02 · Check

      On synthetic traders with a known answer, pure noise is flagged 5.95% of the time, a real 10-point edge needs about 160 trades to be found, and favourite buyers with no edge are flagged 59.25%.

    3. 03 · Data

      A frozen release of 1,266,885,846 fills, read in place with DuckDB, becomes 5,000 accounts and 1,848,270 trades for Nov–Dec 2025.

    4. 04 · Test

      On real accounts the p-values do not lie flat: 1,430 fall below 0.05, and 737 of them belong to the 879 accounts that pay 95¢ or more on average.

    Of 702 accounts that pay 99¢ or more, 613 are flagged skilled. None is flagged anti-skilled.

    Flipping signs builds a null that is symmetric around zero. A 99¢ contract pays +1¢ almost every time and −99¢ about once in a hundred.

    Over 20 events the most likely history has no loss (81.8% of the time), and that is the most extreme history the null can produce.

    With no cap, 26.9% are skilled and 11.1% anti-skilled, the reverse of the paper's Table 1. At an 80¢ cap the two come close: 11.4% and 14.1%.

    Same test, same thresholds. Where the price cap sits decides which side is larger.

    Compared four ways, the flags follow the price paid more than the trader who paid it.

    1. A · Against the paper

      With no cap the replication flags 3.12 times the paper's skilled share and 0.66 times its anti-skilled share; under an 80¢ cap the order flips back to the paper's, with 0.81 skilled per anti-skilled account against the paper's 0.51.

    2. B · Against a trader with no edge

      In five of six price buckets, in both windows, the share flagged skilled sits inside the range a simulated trader with no edge would get; only 80–90¢ is above it, at 42.2% against at most 35.2%.

    3. C · Against the correction

      Benjamini–Hochberg removes 53.1% of the anti-skilled flags but only 10.3% of the skilled ones; under an 80¢ cap it removes 37.8% and 52.3%. It corrects for many tests, not for a null that ignores the price.

    4. D · Where the flags come from

      The 14.5% of buyers who pay 99¢ or more on average produce 47% of all skilled flags in Nov–Dec; in December alone, 19.7% produce 53.4%.

    Verdict

    On these trades the sign-flip test measures the price of the contract before it measures the skill of the trader.

    1. 87.3%

      of accounts that pay 99¢ or more are flagged skilled, close to the 82.7% a trader with no edge gets in simulation at 99¢ and 20 events.

    2. 5 / 6

      price buckets, in both windows, sit inside the zero-edge range: the flags are what the null produces when payoffs are this skewed.

    3. 47%

      of all skilled flags come from 14.5% of buyers, the favourite buyers; correcting with BH barely touches them.

    4. 0.81

      skilled per anti-skilled account under an 80¢ cap, against 2.43 with no cap and 0.51 in the paper: where the price mix is closer to fair, the replication moves toward the paper.

    So a count of "skilled" accounts on Polymarket depends on how many favourites they buy. It does not show that the paper is wrong; it shows that its Table 1 needs the price mix next to it, and that a fair-price null is the test to run next.
